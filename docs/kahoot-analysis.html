Your Kahoot quiz nights: what makes them work

An analysis of the six hand-written nights (20 June, 10 July, 18 July, 1 August, the Friday 90s edition and Laura's Birthday), with my take on what the one-click builder has to copy. Prepared 27 September 2026.

My take in one paragraph. Your nights feel good because of a fixed running order with fresh content in each slot, not because of random variety. Every week has the same shape: a warm TV & Film opener, a long Music round, a light picture round into the break, Sport straight after it, a QI "General Ignorance" round packed with surprises, a silly wordplay run-in and a Double Points finale that ends on a joke. Inside each round, no two questions in a row share a shape. The wrong answers are chosen with real craft, and about a third of the questions have a story behind them. The first auto-built quiz ("The Return!") got this wrong on every count. It was built from pre-made quick-fire sets that repeated one template, with easy, obvious options. The builder now copies your skeleton instead.

1. The skeleton: five of the six nights are the same Kahoot, re-filled

The 20 June, 10 July, 18 July, 1 August and Friday nights are the same Kahoot duplicated with the questions swapped. The round order, the round sizes and even the question type in each slot stay the same week to week.

2. Pacing and flow

  • Difficulty goes up and down; it doesn't climb. A light format round comes every 15–20 questions.
  • The hardest factual stretch (Sport, QI) comes straight after the break, when attention is fresh. It stays playable because most of it is multiple choice.
  • Rounds open warm and close with a signpost or a gag. The first three questions of the night are easy on purpose, so everyone scores early.
  • About 31% of the night is game-show formats (Catchphrase, Wheel of Fortune, Answer Smash, rhymes and the finale). The other 69% is trivia.
What this means for us. A one-click quiz should be a format: fixed rounds in a fixed order, with the game shows rotating week by week. It shouldn't be a random pile of types. That's what the new builder does. There are four formats that take turns, and each keeps your skeleton. One week leans on Wipeout, the next on King of the Hill, the next on Hot Potato and Blockbusters.

Repetition: the same wording template never appears twice in a row in the TV, Music and Sport rounds. The same Kahoot type runs at most 2–3 in a row. In a mixed round every question is a different subject. Sport, for example, runs football ×5–7, then one each of snooker or darts, tennis, boxing, rugby, cricket and the Olympics.

4. How you choose wrong answers

  • Same-set plausibles: Frances / Rosamund / Renesmee / Emmaline. Only knowledge decides.
  • Numeric ladders with the truth at the surprising end: 5,000 / 50,000 / 500,000 tons; 1677 / 1777 / 1877 / 1977.
  • The obvious answer as the trap: Gold for gold medals (they're mostly silver); 100 years for the Hundred Years War (116).
  • A comic decoy next to an absurd truth: "Scotland: no, we haven't got pants under there".
  • Rhyming decoys: Kate Hudson's middle name: Howie / Barry / Larry / Garry.
  • Celebrity decoys for laughs: a singer's famous parent: Alan Shearer / Michael McIntyre / Holly Willoughby / David Cameron.
What this means for us. This is where "The Return!" fell down most. Its quick-fire games recycled each other's answers as options ("Speed", "Taken" and "Total Recall" kept reappearing), so later rows became a clicking contest. The new dealer never shows one row's answer as another row's option. It has also retired 168 pre-made races that recycled their options, until they're rewritten.

5. The "Well I never knew that!" factor

About 40% of your trivia questions carry a surprise or a laugh, and about a third are strong "gasp" moments. That's 80–90% of the QI round and about 25–35% of TV, Music and Sport. A few favourites:

  • Cleopatra lived closer to the Moon landings than to the pyramids.
  • All Tom Cruise's wives were 33 when their marriages ended.
  • Olympic gold medals are mostly silver.
  • Mozart outsold Beyoncé on CD in 2016.
  • Henry VI banned kissing (1439).
  • Cookie Monster's real name is Sid.
  • Paula Radcliffe and the 2005 London Marathon.
  • Bradley Walsh had 2016's best-selling British debut album.
  • Love Is All Around was originally by The Troggs.
  • Gary Lineker was never booked.
What this means for us. Your ask of "at least 15%" is a floor, not the target: your own nights run at about a third. The bank now carries a hand-review flag: 847 questions are marked "wow" on top of the Ripley's and "No Such Thing As a Fish" topics. Every format has a dedicated "Well I Never Knew That!" round drawn only from those. Mixed rounds reserve a quarter of their slots for them.

6. Difficulty

Roughly 35% easy-if-you-know-it, 45% challenging but guessable, 20% "how did you get that?". The hard ones are almost always multiple choice, so they stay guessable. The easy ones stay interesting because of the angle: "Which Beatle led the way across Abbey Road?" rather than "Who were the Beatles?". The picture or clip can be the puzzle, a joke decoy can lighten it, and nostalgia and local pride help. Nothing is nursery-level.

What this means for us. A hand review of 14,000 bank questions found about 3,500 that are nursery-level for an adult room (spider legs, "merci", Peppa Pig, the plural of mouse). They're now excluded from every build. Mixed rounds are picked at about 15% easy, 50% medium and 35% hard on the bank's labels, since those labels run generous. The AI check then re-rates every question honestly and replaces anything too easy.

7. Music rounds

They're about the stories around songs as much as recognising them:

  • covers and originals (Hurt / Nine Inch Nails, The Troggs);
  • band members and lead singers, real names and famous relatives;
  • chart records (White Christmas, Fleetwood Mac's only UK No.1);
  • release years, pop timelines, lyrics and pixelated album covers.

They lean to the 90s and 00s. There are no musicals at all.

8. Wording

  • One natural sentence of 8–20 words. QI questions run to 25–35 words with a set-up. Terse prompts only for media ("Name the band").
  • "&" used freely; "+" only in a sum. That answers your 1% Club note: use "&".
  • British spelling and slang, £ for money, chatty asides ("FOCUS PAUL!"), and Newcastle banter over Sunderland.
  • Typed answers of 1–4 words, with several accepted spellings.

The answer keys contain a few errors, which is worth knowing if these are ever reused:

  • Muhammad Ali's losses are keyed as 1 in one quiz and 5 in another. It's 5.
  • The NASA film is keyed to Tom Hanks. It was Tom Cruise.
  • Mary Berry's birth year is keyed 1926. It's 1935.
  • Dolly Parton is keyed as a singer's grandparent. She's Miley Cyrus's godmother.

The builder fact-checks rather than trusting a key.

10. What changed in the builder

  • One-click formats copy your running order: four of them, taking turns week by week, all editable before building.
  • Chase, King of the Hill, Hot Potato and Blockbusters are dealt question by question from the 13,800 multiple-choice questions. Every row is a different subject and question shape, the rows are full sentences, and no answer is ever recycled as another row's option.
  • Mixed rounds are truly mixed: never two "which flag / which organ / which company / what currency / capital of" in a round, at most two a night, one question per subject, and no answer repeated anywhere in the quiz.
  • Rounds draw from named subjects (Music means pop, rock, number ones and bands, never musicals). Easy North East questions are kept out unless a round is about the North East.
  • Every quiz includes one Alan Shearer question and one Roger question.
  • After building, an AI editor reviews every round against these standards. It replaces what it can, rewrites clumsy wording and lists what still needs your eye in a round-by-round report. You then edit and confirm.
